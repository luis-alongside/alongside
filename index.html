Typical starting portions

Typical starting amounts used by UK eating disorder services. Many children need more than this during recovery. Your team will tell you what your child needs.

Protein (one of these)

  • 1 full chicken breast · 1 fish fillet · 2 eggs
  • 3 slices of meat (each the size of a playing card)
  • 3 sausages · 6 nuggets · 1 medium burger · 3 slices of pizza
  • 4 tablespoons of beans · cheese the size of a small matchbox

Carbohydrates (one of these)

  • 4 heaped serving spoons of rice, pasta or couscous
  • 2 medium boiled potatoes · 2 scoops of mash · 1 large baked potato
  • 2 slices of bread · 1 wrap · 1 bagel

Vegetables

  • 2 serving spoons, or 1 dessert bowl of salad

Cereal

  • 1 mug (200 ml) of cornflakes or similar · or 2 Weetabix, with whole milk

Desserts (one of these)

  • ¼ fruit pie · 1 individual cheesecake · 2 scoops of ice cream · 5 tablespoons of custard

Snacks (each of these is one snack)

  • 1 nut-based cereal bar · 4 biscuits · 1 normal-size chocolate bar
  • 1 scone with butter · 2 pancakes with butter · 4 crackers with 2 cheese triangles
  • Fruit only together with something else, e.g. an apple with 2 tbsp peanut butter

Guide

Practical, evidence-based help for each part of the day.

At the table
Before, during and after a meal

Before

  • Decide the meal and portion yourself. Asking "what do you want?" gives the illness a way in.
  • Serve the plate already filled.
  • Give a short heads-up: "Dinner in 10 minutes."
  • Take three slow breaths. Calm is catching.

During

Helpful to say

  • "I know this is really hard. I'm right here."
  • "This is your medicine. You need all of it."
  • "I can see the illness is shouting at you right now."
  • "Next bite." (calm, repeated, no debate)
  • "Let's not talk about the food. Tell me about…"

Try to avoid

  • Calories, grams, weight, sizes
  • "Healthy" or "unhealthy" foods
  • Comments about anyone's body
  • Bargaining ("just half")
  • Threats, shouting, long explanations
  • Sit with them for the whole meal, and eat together if you can.
  • Many services suggest about 30 minutes for a meal and 15 for a snack.
  • Use light distraction: chat, a card game, a podcast.

After

  • Stay together for 30–60 minutes, doing something calm.
  • Try to avoid bathroom trips straight after eating, and no "walking it off".
  • Comfort them, don't debate: "I know it feels awful. It will get easier."
Compassionate words: five steps
  1. Stay in the moment. "I can see it's taking a while to pick up the fork."
  2. Be curious. "I'm wondering whether this is difficult for you."
  3. Be compassionate. "It looks really hard. I'm sorry."
  4. Be supportive and firm. "I want you to start. Trust me, I know what you need."
  5. Look ahead. "After lunch we'll watch that film."

Try to avoid criticism. Outside mealtimes, keep doing things you both enjoy.

If they refuse
  1. Pause. Lower your voice and sit closer.
  2. Name the illness: "I know it's telling you not to. I'm not going to let it decide."
  3. Repeat the expectation warmly: "You need to finish this. I'll stay with you."
  4. Give it time. Calm persistence often works after a few minutes.
  5. If the meal can't be finished, stay calm, don't punish them, and tell the team.
Behaviours
Hiding or throwing away food

This is driven by the illness, not by bad character. Supervise all meals and snacks for now. If you find hidden food, say calmly: "I found this. We'll have it now together." Tell the team, especially if weight isn't going up.

Eating very slowly, rituals, cutting food small

Prompt gently: "Bigger bites, please." Agree time limits with the team. Serve food so it's harder to pick apart: sauces mixed in, butter already spread.

Exercise and constant movement

Watch for exercise in their room, always standing, or pacing. If the team has restricted activity, keep the rule clear and kind: "Your heart needs rest right now." Offer calm activities instead, and report it if it continues.

Bathroom straight after eating

This can sometimes mean getting rid of food. Encourage a bathroom visit before meals, keep them company afterwards, and tell the team if you're worried. Don't accuse them.

Weight not going up despite eating

Possible reasons include portions still too small, hidden exercise, food thrown away, or very high energy needs during recovery. Share photos of meals with the team and ask: "What should we change this week?"

Anger, tears, "I hate you"

Distress often peaks as eating increases, which usually means you're doing the right thing. Stay calm: "I love you too much to let the illness win." Reconnect later through something unrelated to food.

Bargaining and asking for reassurance

Give one short, kind answer, then move on: "This is the right amount. I'm in charge of the food so you don't have to be."

Body checking and comparing

Keep all body, weight and diet talk out of the home, including about yourselves. Gently redirect, and mention it to the team.

If you find out they are self-harming

Your feelings are valid. Try not to direct them at your child. Ask how they're feeling without leading with your worries. Tell them you love them no matter what. Together, notice what triggers it and plan other ways to cope with strong feelings. If you're worried about their safety, go to A&E.

Food
Principles of eating for recovery
  • 3 meals and 2–3 snacks, with no more than about 3 hours without food.
  • Parents decide what, when and how much. The child's job is to eat it.
  • Carbohydrate at every meal, about half the plate.
  • Fats are essential for the brain and hormones: butter, oil, cheese, whole milk.
  • Go easy on bulky foods like big salads and fizzy drinks. They fill up without enough energy.
  • No diet products: no light, zero, low-fat or sugar-free versions.
  • Drinks: about 2 litres a day, with no more than 2 cups of tea or coffee.
Reintroducing feared foods

Foods the illness has labelled "bad" (chocolate, chips, pizza, cake) need to come back over time, step by step, with your team's guidance. Start small, alongside a safe food, at a calm time of day. Add the foods your child avoids to their profile, and menus will bring in at most one a day.

Early weeks: physical signs to report

Tell the team promptly about swollen ankles or face, prolonged bloating, night sweats, unusual weakness, dizziness, or a racing or fluttering heart.

Understanding
What anorexia is, and what it isn't

Anorexia nervosa is a serious mental illness with physical effects. It isn't a choice or a diet gone too far, and it isn't caused by bad parenting. Starvation itself makes thinking rigid, anxious and food-focused. The Minnesota Starvation Experiment showed this even in healthy adults. Food comes first, and thinking usually eases as weight recovers.

You
Two homes or several carers, one plan
  • Use the same meal plan, portions and rules in every home.
  • Send a short handover each time your child moves between homes.
  • Agree together what to say when they ask for an exception.
  • Keep disagreements between adults, away from the table.
Looking after yourself

This is not your fault. Have one person you can text after a hard meal. Take 10 minutes outside for yourself each day. Notice small wins. Keep some family time that has nothing to do with food.

Urgent help

When in doubt, get help. These are UK numbers.

Call 999 or go to A&E now if they:
  • Faint, collapse or can't be woken properly
  • Have chest pain, or a racing, pounding or irregular heartbeat
  • Are very short of breath, confused or very drowsy
  • Have hurt themselves, or say they plan to end their life
  • Vomit blood

Many NHS eating disorder services also advise A&E if your child has:

  • Refused all drinks for 36 hours
  • Refused all food for 48–72 hours
  • Lost 1 kg or more a week for more than 2 weeks

If they refuse to go and it isn't safe to take them, call 999. If they run away, call 999 and report a missing child. Always follow your own team's advice where it differs.

Call 999
Call your team the same day (or NHS 111 out of hours) if:
  • They're dizzy or light-headed, especially when standing
  • They feel very cold or weak, or have a very slow pulse
  • Their feet, ankles or face are swollen
  • They're being sick, or you suspect they're making themselves sick
  • They talk about self-harm or hopelessness
